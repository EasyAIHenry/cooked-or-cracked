Recently, I spent two hours in a tent talking about play. It might be the most useful conversation I have had in months.

The question on the table was simple. When did play become unproductive?

As children, play was the most natural thing in the world. No goal, no metric, no point to prove. Somewhere on the way to becoming adults, we turned it into something we have to schedule, justify, or earn. We made play productive, which is almost a contradiction in terms.

I got deep into it with Steph and Daemon, ex-Ergotune, and Daemon shared something that reframed the whole evening for me.

Growing up, his father loved to talk. Mostly his own opinions, often about how great China is. Young Daemon kept offering his own views back, not realising his dad was never asking for them. His father wanted to be heard, full stop.

For years, talking to his dad was hard. Then Daemon flipped it. Instead of fighting for airtime, he started asking his father more, prompting him, seeing how far he could take him. He turned a frustrating dynamic into a quiet game, a challenge to draw his dad out.

Years later, he realised that was a form of play. The adaptive kind, where a child finds a way to connect on the only terms available to him.

That sat with me for the rest of the night. Play is how we stay flexible when life refuses to give us what we want. It is bigger than fun.

I run hard most weeks. Rooms like this remind me that the most productive thing I can do is sometimes to stop trying to be productive.

When did you last play with no point to it at all? I would like to know.
